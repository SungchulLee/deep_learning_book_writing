# Gap Sequences

Shell sort generalizes insertion sort by allowing elements to be compared and swapped across large distances, not just adjacent positions. The sequence of distances used — called the **gap sequence** — is the single most important design choice in Shell sort. Different gap sequences yield dramatically different worst-case complexities, ranging from $O(n^2)$ down to $O(n \log^2 n)$ or even better empirically. This page surveys the most important gap sequences, analyzes their complexity bounds, and provides guidance on choosing among them.

## How Gap Sequences Work

Shell sort performs multiple passes over the array. In each pass, elements that are a fixed distance $h$ apart are compared and sorted using insertion sort. The algorithm starts with a large gap and progressively reduces it. When the gap reaches $1$, the final pass is a standard insertion sort — but by then, the array is nearly sorted, so few comparisons are needed.

A **gap sequence** is a decreasing sequence of positive integers $h_1 > h_2 > \cdots > h_t = 1$ that determines the gaps used in each pass. The requirement $h_t = 1$ ensures that the final pass is a standard insertion sort, guaranteeing correctness.

!!! note "Why the Final Gap Must Be 1"
    If the gap sequence does not end with $1$, elements that are not multiples of any gap apart will never be compared, and the output may not be sorted. The final gap of $1$ ensures every pair of adjacent elements is compared.

## Shell's Original Sequence (1959)

Donald Shell's original proposal uses gaps that start at $\lfloor n/2 \rfloor$ and halve at each step:

$$
h_k = \left\lfloor \frac{n}{2^k} \right\rfloor \quad \text{for } k = 1, 2, \ldots
$$

This produces the sequence $\lfloor n/2 \rfloor, \lfloor n/4 \rfloor, \ldots, 2, 1$.

**Worst-case complexity:** $\Theta(n^2)$.

The quadratic worst case occurs on inputs specifically constructed to exploit the fact that elements in even positions and odd positions are never compared until the final gap-1 pass. Despite this poor worst case, Shell's sequence is simple and performs reasonably on random data.

!!! example "Shell's Sequence for n = 16"
    Gaps: $8, 4, 2, 1$.

    - Pass 1 ($h=8$): compare elements 8 positions apart.
    - Pass 2 ($h=4$): compare elements 4 positions apart.
    - Pass 3 ($h=2$): compare elements 2 positions apart.
    - Pass 4 ($h=1$): standard insertion sort on a nearly sorted array.

## Hibbard's Sequence (1963)

Hibbard proposed gaps of the form $2^k - 1$:

$$
h_k = 2^k - 1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad 1, 3, 7, 15, 31, 63, \ldots
$$

**Worst-case complexity:** $\Theta(n^{3/2})$.

The improvement over Shell's sequence comes from the fact that consecutive gaps in Hibbard's sequence are coprime (their GCD is 1). This ensures that elements compared in one pass are interleaved with elements compared in the next, preventing the pathological patterns that cause $O(n^2)$ behavior.

## Knuth's Sequence (1973)

Knuth proposed the sequence generated by

$$
h_k = \frac{3^k - 1}{2} \quad \Rightarrow \quad 1, 4, 13, 40, 121, 364, \ldots
$$

starting from $h_1 = 1$ and stopping at the largest $h_k < n/3$.

**Worst-case complexity:** $\Theta(n^{3/2})$.

Knuth's sequence is easy to compute (each gap is $3h_{k-1} + 1$) and achieves the same $\Theta(n^{3/2})$ worst-case bound as Hibbard's sequence. It is a popular choice due to its simplicity.

## Sedgewick's Sequences (1982, 1986)

Robert Sedgewick proposed two gap sequences with improved bounds.

**1982 sequence:**

$$
h_k = 4^k + 3 \cdot 2^{k-1} + 1 \quad \Rightarrow \quad 1, 8, 23, 77, 281, \ldots
$$

**1986 sequence (interleaved):**

$$
h_k = \begin{cases} 9 \cdot 4^k - 9 \cdot 2^k + 1 & \text{if } k \text{ is even} \\ 2^{k+2}(2^{k+2} - 3) + 1 & \text{if } k \text{ is odd} \end{cases}
$$

producing $1, 5, 19, 41, 109, 209, \ldots$

**Worst-case complexity:** $O(n^{4/3})$.

Sedgewick's sequences achieve the best proven worst-case bound among known gap sequences and perform well in practice.

## Ciura's Sequence (2001)

Marcin Ciura determined an empirically optimal gap sequence through extensive experiments:

$$
1, 4, 10, 23, 57, 132, 301, 701, \ldots
$$

This sequence was found by minimizing the average number of comparisons across random inputs of various sizes. For gaps beyond $701$, Ciura suggested extending by a factor of approximately $2.25$.

**Worst-case complexity:** Unknown (no tight theoretical bound). Empirically, it outperforms all other known sequences on average.

## Why Gap Sequence Choice Matters

The choice of gap sequence affects Shell sort's complexity by orders of magnitude. The fundamental insight is that **coprimality** between consecutive gaps prevents certain pathological input patterns from persisting across passes.

When consecutive gaps share a common factor $d$, elements at positions that are multiples of $d$ are repeatedly compared with each other while elements at non-multiple positions are ignored until later. This creates pockets of unsorted elements that the final pass must handle, leading to quadratic behavior.

Gap sequences where consecutive terms are coprime (like Hibbard's) avoid this problem because each pass interleaves elements from different "streams" of the previous pass, spreading the sorting work more evenly.

!!! warning "Open Problem"
    Finding the gap sequence that minimizes the worst-case number of comparisons for Shell sort remains an open problem. No known gap sequence achieves $O(n \log n)$ worst-case time, and it is unknown whether such a sequence exists. This makes Shell sort one of the few classical algorithms whose optimal configuration is still unresolved.
